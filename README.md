# OpenRacer

A racing simulator that runs in the browser. It simulates the whole car (a body on quarter-car suspension, tyres with load, pressure, camber, heat and wear, a clutch, a gearbox and differentials), races you against AI cars, works with a keyboard, a gamepad or a racing wheel with force feedback, and has a car editor, a track editor and a tuning menu to make your own cars and tracks.

**Play it and read the docs: [openracer.eu](https://openracer.eu)** (the game is at [openracer.eu/game](https://openracer.eu/game/), the documentation at [openracer.eu/docs.html](https://openracer.eu/docs.html)). The source of the site is in `site/`.

**Join the community on [Discord](https://discord.gg/JFgHVFUagF)** to share setups, cars and tracks, ask for help and talk about development.

Released under the GPLv3 License. Some models come from others under their own licence (Creative Commons): see [CREDITS.md](CREDITS.md).

## About this fork
This is a fork of [digi-chris/OpenRacer](https://github.com/digi-chris/OpenRacer) by Chris Barnard, the open-source initiative that began the game: the demo Mustang, its first track and the first version of the driving model. This fork takes it much further: the vehicle physics, the race format, the input (including racing wheels with force feedback), the editors, the engine sound and the local garage were built here.

The fork was developed with AI, in Claude Code, together with **Claude Fable** and **Claude Sonnet 5.5** from Anthropic. The commit log shows how it grew, step by step, and the commits made by the models say so.

What is new in every version is in [CHANGELOG.md](CHANGELOG.md). The version of the game is `OpenRacer.VERSION` in `web/js/Version.js` (shown in the main menu and at the bottom of the settings menu). Raise it when a car or a track made with the game would not work the same on an older version.

## Running locally
OpenRacer is a static web app with a small helper for the lists of cars and tracks. Run it with

    ./start-server.sh        # optionally pass a port, default 8000; add --local to keep it to this computer

With `--https` it serves over https, with a certificate that is made on the first start (`tools/.certs`, for localhost and the address on the network): the browser warns once, because no authority made it; choose to go on. With [mkcert](https://github.com/FiloSottile/mkcert) installed (`mkcert -install` once) the certificate is trusted and there is no warning. Some things of the browser only work on https or on localhost: the clipboard, the gamepad on some browsers and the motion of a phone.

The server is also reachable on the local network: it prints the address (for example http://192.168.1.51:8000) for a phone, a tablet or another computer. Those can play and read; only this computer can write cars and tracks to the folders (the others keep them in the garage of their own browser). The firewall of the computer may ask for permission.

This serves the `web` folder (`tools/serve.py`) and opens http://localhost:8000 in your browser (WebGL required). Nothing is cached, so after a `git pull` a normal page refresh is enough and the server never needs a restart. Opening `index.html` directly does not work, because the game loads its files. A plain web server (`cd web && python3 -m http.server 8000`) works too: the standard cars and tracks are then the ones in `web/cars/index.json` and `web/tracks/index.json` (which `serve.py` keeps up to date), and what a player makes or imports goes to the garage of their browser (see *Your own cars and tracks*).

## Main menu, cars and tracks
The game starts with a main menu to choose a car and a track (**Esc** in the game has a button to come back to it). Both lists are made by looking at the folders: every `web/cars/<id>/car.json` is a car and every `web/tracks/<id>/track.json` is a track, so a new folder shows up at the next refresh (or with *Look for new cars and tracks*). The choice is in the address (`index.html?car=<id>&track=<id>`).

A track folder has `track.json` (name, the track geometry and scene files, the folder of the textures, `start` with x, z and heading, and the names of the road pieces that make a lap) and the files it names. `web/tracks/circuit` is the track of the demo.

## Your own cars and tracks
The game can be hosted as plain files (no `serve.py`), with the cars and tracks of `web/cars` and `web/tracks` as the set that comes with it. Players add their own on their side, in the **Garage** (a link on the home screen of the main menu, with *Load*, *New* and *Import*; the editors open from there); nothing is uploaded to the server. The garage lives in the browser (IndexedDB) and holds cars and tracks as records: the `car.json` or `track.json` plus the files of the model (`web/js/Garage.js`). Three sources make the lists of the menu (`Catalog.js`): the server (`api/cars` with `serve.py`, otherwise `cars/index.json`), the garage, and a folder on the PC; a car or track of the garage replaces the one of the server with the same id, so a tuned copy of a standard car keeps its id and its lap records. Items that are not of the server have a badge in the menu (*my garage* or *folder*). Their files are given to the loaders as blob URLs (`Garage.resolve` for a file of the car or track, `Garage.manager` for the `.bin` and textures a glTF refers to).

- **Saving.** The car editor and the track editor (also the glb it makes for a drawn track, and the AI racing line it records) save through the server when it has the API (`start-server.sh`), and otherwise to the garage, and say which (`Garage.store`). A tuning saved to a standard car on a plain host makes a garage record with only the settings: the model stays the one of the server.
- **Zip.** In the garage dialog a car or track is exported as `<id>.car.zip` or `<id>.track.zip` (`<id>/car.json` plus the files; `web/js/Zip.js`, a small store-only writer and a reader that also inflates deflated zips), and a zip is imported the same way: one with `car.json` or `track.json` at its root or in one folder, so a zipped folder from `web/cars` works too. The id is the folder name in the zip.
- **A folder on the PC.** In Chrome and Edge (the File System Access API) a folder can be linked: its `cars/<id>/car.json` and `tracks/<id>/track.json` (or the folder itself when it holds cars directly) are read every time the menu opens, nothing is copied. The browser asks once per session to allow reading it; the dialog has the button for that.
- **Space.** The dialog shows how much the browser lets the site keep (usually gigabytes, Safari about a gigabyte, and Safari forgets it after seven days without a visit, so export what matters). The server holds only the game and its standard set.

## Making a track
A track is `web/tracks/<id>/track.json` with its files. Two formats: the old one of the demo (`geometry`, `scene`, `textures`; laps counted by `checkpoints`, the names of road pieces) and a glTF/glb model: `model` (the file in the folder), `surfaces.meshes` (the name of a part → `road`, `track01` (pit lane), `grass`, `ground`, `scenery` or `decor`; what is not mentioned is scenery), `start` (x, z, heading in radians from +x towards +z) and `gates`, lines across the road (x, z, heading, width) that the car has to drive through in order for a lap; the first is the finish line. Drivable parts have their own grip, scenery is a wall for the car where it is steep, decor is only to look at. The model is in metres, y up.

A track can have an `environment` (all of it optional, with defaults; a glTF track always gets at least a sky): `sky_top` and `sky_horizon` (colours of the sky), `sky_image` (a picture of the whole sky, 2:1 equirectangular, in the folder of the track), `fog` (`enabled`, `color`, `near`, `far` in metres), `sun` (`azimuth` in degrees from +x towards +z, `elevation`, `intensity`, `color`; it also sets the direction of the shadow of the car) and `ambient` (`color`, `intensity`). A track of the old format keeps the sky of its model unless it has an environment.

**Drawing a track without a model.** *New track* in the track editor starts a design: a smooth closed loop through nodes. Click on the ground to add a node (it goes between the two nodes it is nearest to), drag a node to move it over the ground and its arrow up or down for hills; every node has a width of the road. The road gets red and white kerbs and grass beside it, and the ground is a landscape that joins the shoulders: under the road it lies just below the lowest road nearby (a road that crosses over another one becomes a bridge), further away it follows the heights of the road around it and rolls gently on its own. The *Prefabs* tab of the drawer at the bottom has walls, barriers, tyre stacks and tyre walls, cones, trees, bushes, houses, a grandstand, lamp posts and sign boards: pick one and click on the ground to put one down, or drag a line on the ground to make a row of them that follows the line (the spacing can be set; the points of the line can be dragged afterwards). A prefab is moved by dragging it, and sits on whatever is under it (the ground, the road or another prefab); a single one has a ring to turn it. Delete removes the selection, Ctrl+D duplicates it, Ctrl+Z and Ctrl+Shift+Z undo and redo, Esc deselects and then goes back to *Look*. Everything has a tileable texture (`web/textures`, made by `tools/make-textures.py`: asphalt, concrete, gravel, grass, dirt, sand, brick, plaster, roof tiles, wood, metal, rubber, leaves, bark), tiled in metres so it repeats seamlessly over a road of any length and a wall of any size; the *Track* tab chooses the texture of the road, of the shoulders beside it and of the ground, and a wall, barrier, house or grandstand has its own choice. The checkbox *colours of the surfaces* shows the surfaces as colours instead. Saving writes the track as a glb (`<id>.glb`, made by the editor, with the textures in it as JPEG; a row is one mesh per material) plus the design itself (`design` in `track.json`: nodes, prefabs, rows and textures), so the track can be opened and changed again; the game loads it like any glTF track. Walls, houses, trees and the like are scenery (the car hits them), bushes, cones and signs only decor.

The **track editor** at http://localhost:8000/tools/track-editor.html (a button in the main menu; it needs `start-server.sh`) does all of this:

1. Pick the `.glb` (or `.gltf` with its `.bin` and textures). The parts are listed with a guess of their surface from their names; click a part in the view to find it, change the surface per part, or for all parts that match the filter. *Show the surfaces in colour* shows what you set.
2. *Grid box*: click on the road and drag in the direction that the car faces; every click places the next box of the start grid, up to eight (`grid` in track.json: box 1 is the start of the player, 2 to 8 are where the opponents of a race stand; a track from before the grid has its `start` as box 1). The boxes are drawn on the road in the game as two white side lines joined by a line across the front (the back is open), without numbers. *Add gate*: the same, for every gate; *Gate 1 at the start* puts the finish line just in front of the start. *Pit box*: click on the road (or beside it) and drag in the direction that the car faces: a box of 4 by 8 m for a player, up to eight (`pit.boxes` in track.json: box 1 is the player's, 2 to 8 are the opponents' in the order of the grid); it is yellow and see-through in the game, so that everybody knows which box is theirs. A car that stands still with its wheels inside its box is serviced (fuel and tyres). *Pit line*: click on the road in the order that a car drives (the first point on the racing line where the pit lane starts, through the middle of every box, the last point back on the racing line; `pit.line`): the AI follows this path to its box, slows down for its start and brakes for its stop, and rejoins the racing line at its end, so it does not follow the racing line into a wall. Without a pit line the AI drives straight to its box. A track from before the boxes can keep its older pit area (`pit.polygon`, with the road under it as a pit lane); the Pit boxes card can take it away. *View from above* helps on a big track.
3. *Sky and light*: colours of the sky (or a picture of it), fog, and the direction, height, strength and colour of the sun, with a live preview (fog is only seen in the game). Switch off *Show the surfaces in colour* to see the real colours in this light.
4. *Save the track*: it is written to `web/tracks/<id>/` and is in the main menu. Choose a track in the list to change it (name, start, gates, surfaces, or import a new model); a track of the old format can be opened too to change its start and gates (adding gates replaces its road-piece checkpoints), and a track can be deleted from the same page. Parts that the car drives on have to be separate parts of the model for the surface to be set per part, and it is best to split the road into pieces only if you want different grip on them: laps are counted with gates.

## Making a car
`web/cars/<id>/car.json` describes a car: the model, the size, the chassis (mass, wheelbase, tracks, weight distribution, height of the centre of gravity, tyre diameter, drag, steering lock), the drivetrain (rear or front wheel drive, gear ratios, reverse, final drive), the engine (idle, rev limit, inertia, torque curve in `[rpm, Nm]`) and the brakes (`brakes.front/rear`, Nm per wheel; the tuning menu has them as the total and the **brake bias**, the share of the front). The game comes with `go-kart-250cc`, `hyundai-i30`, `openracer-kart`, `openracer-muscle`, `sports-motorcycle-600cc`, `stadium-truck` and `supermoto-700` (`web/cars/index.json`); the Mustangs of the demo are gone.

The **car editor** at http://localhost:8000/tools/car-editor.html makes such a car from a glTF or glb file, for example from Blender (it needs `start-server.sh`, which writes the files):

1. Pick the `.glb` (or the `.gltf` with its `.bin` and textures). The editor shows the model, the arrow is the front of the car. Turn it if it faces another way, and press *Fit*: the origin of the model has to be at the height of the wheel axles, in the middle between the axles.
2. The wheels have to be separate parts of the model, because they steer and spin. The game takes the wheelbase, the tracks and the tyre size from the wheels of the model, and puts the body over them (whatever the origin of the model is), so the car looks in the game the way it looks in the editor; saving writes the same values to `car.json`. Select the parts of each wheel (*Find the wheels* does this by their names and places). The Wheels card has two choices: **Dynamic wheels** (the default for a new car: the rims and tyres of the wheel library, or the demo wheel, stand where the Chassis card puts them, measured on the wheel parts of the model when it is loaded, and the game takes those parts off the model) and **Wheels from model** (the wheel parts of the model steer and spin). A rim and a tyre of the wheel library (below) can take the place of the wheel parts of an axle. The wheelbase, the tracks, the tyre diameter and the size can be taken from the model.
3. Set the cameras: the *Layers* panel at the right lists the cameras, the model and the wheels; select a camera (or the model) and drag the arrows of the gizmo in the view to move it, the fields follow, and a thumbnail shows what the selected camera sees (the dots in the view are the cameras), the mass, the gears, the engine (a torque table with a chart, or generate a typical curve from the peak power), and the brakes.
4. To update the model of a car that exists, choose the car in the list and pick the new `.glb`: the transform, the wheels (found by the names of their parts) and the material changes (by name) are kept where the new model still fits them, the rest of the car (chassis, cameras, engine, sound) is not touched, and the editor says if the wheelbase, the track or the tyre of the new model differ from the car. The old model file is removed when you save.
5. *Save the car*: it is written to `web/cars/<id>/` and is in the main menu. Choosing an existing car in the list at the top loads its configuration; saving overwrites it (change the id to save a copy). A car can also be deleted from the same page.

The materials of a glTF model are used as they are (physically based: base colour, metalness, roughness, normal, occlusion and emissive maps, clearcoat, transmission). They are lit by the surroundings of the car: the cube map around the car is filtered by roughness and used as the lighting (image based lighting), so paint, glass and chrome reflect the track and the sky. The *Tone mapping* option of the settings menu (Neutral, AgX or ACES) gives a picture closer to Blender's; it is off by default, because it changes the colours of the track too. The car editor shows the model in a studio for the same reason.

A model does not always arrive the way it looked in Blender: a shader that the glTF exporter does not know, such as the glass of many models, ends up as a plain opaque white metal. The *Materials* part of the car editor lists the materials of the model and sets them right with a preset (glass, car paint, chrome, rubber, plastic, brushed metal) or by hand (colour, metalness, roughness, opacity, clear coat, reflection), with a live preview. The changes are saved with the car (`materials` in `car.json`). Glass that arrives opaque is set to see-through glass as a suggestion.

A part of the model called `steeringwheel` turns with the steering (about its own middle, around the axis that is found from its shape: the direction in which the part is flat; `steering_wheel.degrees` in `car.json`, 450 by default, is the turn at full lock each way, set in the car editor). Parts in the same part are turned with it, so keep the wheel, its spokes and its hub together in it.

**Moving suspension and steering** (`SuspensionRig.js`, optional: a model without these parts is shown as before, and the physics does not change). Parts of the model with these names move with the suspension and the steering of the physics (the travel of every wheel from the quarter car, the steering angle). The corner is `FL`, `FR`, `RL` or `RR` at the end of the name; case and other characters do not matter. Model the car at rest on its springs, and keep every part a child of the body (not of a wheel): the part moves from where it is modelled, and its **origin is its pivot**.

| Name | What it is | Origin (pivot) | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| `susp_upperarm_FL`, `susp_lowerarm_FL` | a wishbone, a link | at the hinge on the chassis (between the two inboard bushings) | swings about the axis along the car with the travel of the wheel; its free end reaches the wheel |
| `susp_upright_FL` | the upright, hub carrier, knuckle | at the middle of the wheel | goes up and down with the wheel and turns with the steering (it does not spin, so do not make it a wheel part) |
| `susp_spring_FL`, `susp_damper_FL`, `susp_strut_FL` (or `coilover_cylinder_RL`, `damper_cylinder_RL`) | a spring, a damper, a strut | at the end on the body (the top mount) | points to its other end and gets shorter and longer with the travel. With parts inside it (children named `..._piston_RL`, `..._coil_RL`) the cylinder keeps its length: the piston slides along it with the travel and the coil gets shorter and longer about its own origin |
| `steer_rack` | the steering rack | anywhere on it | slides across the car with the steering (`steering.rack_travel` in `car.json`, m per radian of the front wheels, 0.1 by default; negative slides it the other way) |
| `steer_tierod_FL` | a tie rod | at the end on the rack | its other end sits on the upright and follows it, so it swings and stretches |
| `susp_axle_rear` (or `_front`, `solid_axle_rear`, `rear_solid_differential`) | a live axle | at its middle | goes up and down with its two wheels and rolls with the difference |
| `susp_trailingarm_RL` (or `trailing_arm_RL`, `upper_trailing_arm_RL`) | a trailing arm (of a live axle or a trailing-arm suspension) | at the hinge on the chassis | swings about the axis across the car with the travel of its wheel |
| `susp_panhard_rear` (or `_front`) | a Panhard rod | at the end on the chassis | its other end sits on the axle and follows it up, down and in roll |
| `susp_driveshaft_RL` | a drive shaft (half shaft) of an independent rear | at the differential | its other end is the middle of the wheel: it swings and stretches with the travel |
| `brake_caliper_FL` (or `caliper_FL`, `brake_FL`) | a brake caliper | anywhere | goes with the wheel and steers with it, but does not spin (so do not make it a wheel part; the disc is a wheel part and spins) |
| `sprocket_RL` (or `chainring_RL`, `spin_RL`; a motorcycle: `rear_sprocket`) | a sprocket or chain ring | anywhere | spins with the wheel of that corner, but is not a wheel part: it lies outside the tyre and would make the wheel bigger |
| `rear_spring` (or `rear_shock`, `rear_damper`, `shock_rear`; a motorcycle) | the rear shock | anywhere | its higher end stays on the frame, its lower end goes round with the swingarm: the part is turned and stretched between the two |
| `chain_link` with `front_sprocket` and `rear_sprocket` | the drive chain, from one link | the link anywhere on the chain, the front sprocket on the engine | the link is drawn as many times as the chain is long, along the two strands and round the sprockets (`Chain.js`): it moves with the rear wheel and turns the front sprocket; the radii come from the sprockets, the pitch from the link |

**Gearbox: a manual, a manual with an automatic clutch (IMT), an automated manual (AMT), a dual clutch, an automatic with a torque converter, or a CVT** (the Drivetrain card of the car editor, `drivetrain.gearbox`: `"gears"`, `"imt"`, `"amt"`, `"dct"`, `"torque-converter"` or `"cvt"`; the tuning menu has it too; the type decides whether the driver shifts or the car does, see *Driving*). The dual clutch (DCT) has the gears and the shifting of the others, but the next gear waits on its other clutch: a shift is a hand-over of 0.15 s at most (`shift_time`, capped) in which the drive never stops (the clutch keeps 40 % and goes to full as the engine is pulled to the new speed), with a short cut of the throttle on an upshift and a blip on a downshift. The torque converter keeps the gears and the shifting of the automatic but puts a fluid coupling in place of the clutch: the pump on the engine takes C·ω²·(1 − SR) (SR the speed ratio of turbine to pump; C from `tc_stall_rpm`, 35 % of the rev limit by default: the engine's peak torque holds it at that speed with the car still), the turbine gives that times the torque ratio (`tc_ratio`, 1.9 at stall, 1 from a speed ratio of 0.85: the push off the line), a lock-up clutch closes above 25 km/h once the slip is small (`tc_lockup`), the drive stays on through a shift, and at idle the coupling is a sixth of itself, so the car creeps gently and the brakes hold it. A CVT has no gears: its ratio goes smoothly between the first and the last of the gear ratios, chosen to hold the engine at the speed the throttle asks for, from idle to `cvt_rpm` at full throttle (85 % of the rev limit when not set), a little above idle off the throttle and more on the brakes (engine braking); the belt moves at a limited pace (`cvt_rate`, 1.8: the ratio changes by at most a factor e^1.8 a second). The HUD shows D (R, N; P with the wheels locked): the paddles go R, N, D, P, and with the H-pattern shifter the first gate is D and the second is P.

**Parts library: a suspension and a driveline from loose parts** (`PartsLibrary.js`, the *Suspension and driveline* card of the car editor). Instead of modelling the suspension into every car, pick a type for the front and for the rear in the car editor (double wishbone, MacPherson strut, live axle on trailing arms, live axle on leaf springs; *From the model* keeps the parts of your own model, named as above), where the engine is (front, middle, rear) and the drive (front, rear, all, in the Drivetrain card), and the game builds the suspension and the driveline from glTF files in `web/parts/<type>/<part>.glb`, one set per type, placed on *mounts*: points in the frame of the car that the card shows as gizmos, one at each end of a stretched part (where it sits on the body and where its other end goes: the ball joint, the seat of the spring, the end of the arm on the axle) (W moves, E turns; the left side, the right is its mirror; a default from the wheels, Reset goes back to it). **Edit origins** in the card is a mode in which a click in the view picks only these points, through everything (the model is in the way otherwise); Escape leaves it, and the *Save the car* button at the bottom of the view saves and leaves. The parts get the names of the table above, so they move with the travel and the steering like the parts of a model, and the physics does not change. A propshaft runs from the gearbox to every driven axle whose differential is more than 40 cm from it (to a live axle it follows the axle), an independent driven axle gets a differential and two driveshafts. The files (`tools/make-parts.py` writes plain placeholders of all of them; replace them with your own models of the same names):

| Folder | Files | Mounts |
|---|---|---|
| `double-wishbone` | `upperarm`, `lowerarm`, `upright`, `coilover`, `tierod` | upper_hinge, upper_ball, lower_hinge, lower_ball, spring_top, spring_bottom, tierod_inner, tierod_outer, rack, rack_end (front) |
| `macpherson` | `lowerarm`, `strut`, `upright`, `tierod` | lower_hinge, lower_ball, strut_top, strut_bottom, tierod_inner, tierod_outer, rack, rack_end (front) |
| `live-trailing` | `axle`, `trailingarm`, `uppertrailingarm`, `coilover`, `damper`, `panhard` | axle_end, lower_hinge, lower_arm_end, upper_hinge, upper_arm_end, spring_top, spring_bottom, damper_top, damper_bottom, panhard, panhard_axle |
| `live-leaf` | `axle`, `leafspring`, `damper` | axle_end, leaf_front, leaf_rear, damper_top, damper_bottom |
| `steering` | `rack` | |
| `driveline` | `engine`, `gearbox`, `differential`, `propshaft`, `driveshaft` | engine, gearbox, diff_front, diff_rear, prop_front_start, prop_front_end, prop_rear_start, prop_rear_end (the two ends of a propshaft, at the gearbox and at the differential or the axle) |

How to model a part: a *stretched* part (an arm, a trailing arm, a coilover, a damper, a strut, a tie rod, the Panhard rod, the propshaft, a driveshaft) has its origin at the end on the body (the hinge, the top mount, the gearbox) and is 1 m long along +Y to its other end; the game aims it at its other end and scales it along its length, the thickness stays. A coilover or a strut may hold children named `<part>_piston` (its origin at the far end) and `<part>_coil`: the cylinder keeps its length, the piston slides and the coil gets shorter. The axle has its origin at its middle and is 1 m long along X (scaled to twice the distance of its end mount, `axle_end`, which moves along the axle only); the leaf spring its origin at its middle, where it sits on the axle, 1 m long along Z (scaled from the front hanger to the rear). A *rigid* part (the upright, the engine, the gearbox, a differential) is at its own size with its origin at its mount. The rack has its origin at its middle and is 1 m wide along X: its end mount (`rack_end`, which moves along the rack only and goes with the rack's middle) sets its width, twice the distance from the middle. Everything is modelled for the left side (+x); the right side is the mirror image. A missing file is skipped and named in the card. Every part has an eye in the card: orange is placed from the library, grey is hidden (`"hidden": ["engine", "gearbox"]` of the axle or the driveline in `car.json`), for a car whose own model has that part. In `car.json`: `"suspension": { "front": { "type": "double-wishbone", "mounts": { "lower_hinge": { "position": [x, y, z], "rotation": [x, y, z] } } }, "rear": { ... } }` and `"driveline": { "engine": "front", "mounts": { ... } }` (a turn of a stretched part's mount is a roll about its length).

**Wheel library: rims and tyres to choose from** (`WheelLibrary.js`, the *Wheels and steering* card of the car editor and the *Tyres* group of the tuning menu). A catalogue that you add to yourself, `web/wheels/wheels.json`, lists rims and tyres, each with its own glTF file: `{ "rims": [ { "id": "alloy-17", "name": "Alloy 17\"", "file": "rims/alloy-17.glb", "offset": 0 } ], "tyres": [ { "id": "sport-245-40-18", "name": "Sport 245/40 R18", "file": "tyres/sport-245-40-18.glb", "width": 0.245, "diameter": 0.653, "shell": { "rings": 3, "segments": 36, "inner_ratio": 0.7, "carcass": 0 } } ] }`. Model a rim or a tyre with its origin at the middle of the wheel, on the axle, where it is mounted; the wheel turns about X and the tread lies in the Y-Z plane; for the *left* side of the car (+X is outwards, the face of the rim), the game turns it half a turn for the right side; at real size, in metres: the diameter and the width of a tyre and the diameter of a rim are measured from the file (`width` and `diameter` in the catalogue override the measure), `flip: true` turns a file round that was modelled for the right side, and `offset` of a rim (m) moves the rim and the tyre outwards, the track with them. A rim or a tyre whose file is missing is reported under the lists in the car editor and in the tuning menu after the car is built, and the wheels of the model are used. Pick a rim and a tyre per axle: they take the place of the wheel parts of the model of that axle (their middle is the hub; a car without wheel parts gets them at the wheelbase and the tracks of `car.json`), the other axle keeps its own wheels; `car.json`: `"wheels": { ..., "library": { "front": { "rim": "alloy-17", "tyre": "sport-245-40-18" }, "rear": { ... } } }`. **The shell of the tyre is a property of the tyre**: the rings and nodes of the tread, the radius of the inner shell (without `inner_ratio` the rim that is mounted gives it) and the carcass are set once in the catalogue and go with the tyre to every car that mounts it, so a car on library tyres has nothing to set in `tyres.shell`; the shell sliders of the tuning menu then show the tyre's values greyed out, with a line under the tyres that gives the shell of the front and of the rear (with a library tyre on one axle only, the sliders work for the other axle), and front and rear tyres can have different shells. Choosing a rim or a tyre in the tuning menu builds the car again, as a change of the suspension does. `tools/make-wheels.py` writes a set of plain examples (steel, alloy and beadlock rims, road, sport, semi-slick, slick, off-road and motorcycle tyres) and the catalogue; your own entries in the catalogue are kept when it is run again.

`web/examples/suspension-rig.glb` (made by `tools/make-rig-example.py`) is such a test car: a frame, four wheels, double wishbones with uprights, springs and tie rods at the front, a rack, a live axle at the rear on two trailing arms and a Panhard rod with springs and dampers, every part named and pivoted the way the game wants it; import it in Blender to see the setup, or load it in the car editor as the model of a car. Both the tuning menu and the card show only the settings that apply (a CVT's engine speed only with a CVT, a limited slip's settings only with a limited slip, the turbo's lag only with a turbo, and so on). The cards of the car editor are the cards of the tuning menu (the same table, `CarTuning.js`, so the two always agree). The car editor has a test bench for it at the bottom of the view: **Drop car** lets the car fall on its wheels from a height, with a *Tilt* (rolled and a little nose down, so it lands on one wheel first); **Full test** runs a drop from 1 m at 5°, the engine in neutral to the rev limiter, a metre forwards and back, the steering to the left lock and the right lock, and then full throttle into a wall ahead, with the physics of the game; **Crash test** drives the car into a wall at a speed and angle you set, or pushes it where you click (*Apply force*) (the model is built the way the game builds it, with the suspension rig; *Stop test* goes back to the model); **Drive** lets you drive the car over the floor with the keyboard (arrows or WASD, space the handbrake, R or *Reset car* puts it back at the start; the gearbox is automatic until you shift with X/E and Z/Q, then it is sequential, with reverse below first at a standstill), the camera following. A test car needs no more than a frame, four wheels (as wheel parts, set in the car editor) and these parts; the car editor's Layers panel shows how many rig parts a model has. In Blender: set the origin of each part to its pivot (*Set Origin → 3D Cursor* with the cursor at the hinge), keep the parts separate objects under the body, apply the rotation and the scale, and export with +Y up and the car facing +Z as usual.

`web/cars/stadium-truck/` is an example car for long travel (an off-road car, a stadium truck or a trophy truck): its `car.json` and its model, `trophy-truck.glb` (the bare model of `tools/make-stadium-truck.py`, which uses `tools/glbtools.py`), to model around: a tub with a cab, a hood, a bed, fenders over the 0.9 m tyres, a roll cage, double wishbones at all four wheels with uprights, two coilovers (a damper and a spring) a corner, a rack with tie rods, drive shafts at the rear, brake calipers and a steering wheel, all named and pivoted as the game wants them (the origin is at the height of the axles, midway between them). The suspension of a car of this kind is set with `chassis`: `suspension_length` (m, 0.28 by default: the room for the travel, from the mount of the spring to the middle of the wheel at rest, which must be more than the travel), `suspension_travel` (m, how far the wheel goes in before the bump stop), `spring_front` and `spring_rear` (a soft spring, 20000 N/m, gives a long droop as well: the wheel goes out as far as the weight pushes the spring in), `spring_progression` (0 is a linear spring, 1 makes the spring twice as stiff at the end of the bump travel; the ride height does not change) and `damping_rebound` (how much stiffer the damper is going out than going in, 1.7 by default). All of them are in the tuning menu too. The stadium truck has 0.60 m of room, 0.38 m of bump travel, springs of 20 and 22 N/mm, a progression of 1.2 and a rebound of 2.2: a drop from 3 m uses 0.22 m of the travel and does not bottom out.

In Blender a car that looks towards -Y (the front view) exports as it should; the model needs a name per part for the wheels to be found. glTF files with Draco or Meshopt compression are not supported.

## Driving model
The car is simulated after the architecture of [Programming vehicles in games](https://wassimulator.com/blog/programming/programming_vehicles_in_games.html) (`web/js/Vehicle.js`, `Wheel.js`, `Ground.js`, with no 3D dependencies), three parts that are coupled: the driveline, the tyres and the chassis.

* **Chassis**: a rigid body in 3D (position, orientation, velocity, angular velocity, mass and the inertia of a box). It pitches, rolls, can jump and can turn over. Gravity, air drag, downforce or lift per axle (`chassis.downforce_front` and `downforce_rear` in N per (m/s)², negative for lift; `downforce` alone is split by the weight distribution; it presses at the axle, so the balance of the car changes with the speed) and the forces of the four wheels act on it; walls push on it with impulses at the point that hit them (`Collision.js`). The front wheels steer with Ackermann (`chassis.ackermann`, 0.6 by default: the inner wheel turns more than the outer one, most of the way to the full geometry), so a slow bend does not scrub the inner tyre.
* **Suspension**: a quarter car at every corner, or none: *No suspension (a kart)* in the car editor and *Suspension: None* in the Chassis group of the tuning menu (`chassis.unsprung`) fix the wheels to the frame, the load of the tyre goes straight into the body and the tyre is the only spring (the springs, dampers, travel and ride height do nothing then; the roll stiffness and the roll centres still work). **Anti-dive, anti-squat and anti-lift** (`chassis.anti_dive`, `anti_squat`, `anti_lift_front`, `anti_lift_rear`, 0 to 1, in the Chassis group of the tuning menu): the share of the pitch of the brake and drive forces that the linkage takes, as the roll centres do for the lateral force: that share of the force along a tyre is passed to the body higher up, so it pitches less on its springs, and goes to the tyre loads at once; the total load transfer stays Fx h / L. Anti-dive is the front under the brakes, anti-squat the rear under power, anti-lift the rear under the brakes and the front under power; 0 when not set, and not for a motorcycle (its fork and swingarm have their geometry). The wheel with its tyre, brake and hub is a mass of its own (`chassis.unsprung_mass`, 35 kg) that hangs on the spring and the damper (and an anti-roll bar, so the roll stiffness has the front share of `chassis.roll_front`) and stands on the tyre, which is a stiff spring with a little damping (`tyre_rate` 220 kN/m, `tyre_damping`). The road is found with rays from the mount of the wheel (`Ground.js`: the drivable triangles of the track in a grid, with their height, slope and surface), sampled ahead of and behind the middle of the wheel so that the rim touches a kerb or an edge where it reaches it first and rolls on to it gradually. The road pushes the tyre in, the tyre pushes the wheel, the wheel pushes the spring, the spring pushes the body: the load on the tyre (what it grips with) is the tyre's own, and a sharp bump reaches the body through the spring and a digressive damper (past a knee its force grows only slowly, the blow-off of a real damper), so a kerb is a thump and not a blow. Weight transfer is therefore not a formula: it follows from the springs, the centre of gravity height and the accelerations. Settings in `car.json`: `chassis.spring_front/spring_rear` (N/m at the wheel), `damping_front/damping_rear` (damping ratio), `roll_front`, the travel before the bump stop `suspension_travel` (m, 0.09 by default; a slider in the car editor, 1–80 cm, which also works on the test bench while it runs), and the ride height per axle `ride_front/ride_rear` (m from the car as made, positive is higher: it raises the body and its centre of gravity there, and the two together give rake; in the tuning menu in mm).
* **Tyres**: the tyre is one body of nodes and beams (see *Tyre model* below): its grip, collision and deformation come from the nodes. With load sensitivity, a relaxation length, **tyre pressure** (`tyres.pressure_front/pressure_rear`, bar, 2.2 by default: around it the tyre grips best; lower it is softer against the slip angle, rolls heavier and runs hotter, higher it is stiffer with a smaller patch, and its stiffness as a spring follows), **toe** (`chassis.toe_front/toe_rear`, degrees, positive is toe-in: the wheels point a little inwards, which calms the rear and, as toe-out at the front, sharpens the turn-in) and camber: a wheel that leans (its static camber, `chassis.camber_front` and `camber_rear` in degrees, negative is the top in, and the lean of the body in roll, `roll_camber`) pushes towards the side it leans to. The force along the wheel acts on the chassis at the contact patch, the force across it at the roll centre of the axle (`roll_center_front` and `roll_center_rear`, m above the road): the part of the load transfer below the roll centre goes through the linkage at once and presses the outer tyre down, only the rest rolls the body on its springs. All of it is in the tuning menu. A tyre grips less per Newton the more it is loaded, and the rear tyres are a little stiffer against the slip angle: the car understeers at the limit, which keeps it stable (a small steering movement at 150 km/h does not start a spin).
* **Driveline**: engine (with inertia, idle, rev limiter and stalling) → friction clutch → gearbox → differential → wheels. The clutch can only transmit a limited torque, so it grips, slips or slides depending on the pedal and the load. Each wheel is an inertia whose speed follows from the engine, brakes and tyre, so wheels can spin or lock up. The drive is RWD, FWD or AWD (`drivetrain.drive`). The differential between the wheels of an axle (`drivetrain.diff`) is *open* (the same torque to both wheels, so the wheel with the least grip decides: a spinning inner wheel loses the power), *lsd* (limited slip: a preload `diff_preload` in Nm plus a share of the driven torque, `diff_power` under power and `diff_coast` off the throttle, 0 to 1) or *welded* (both wheels always turn at the same speed). An AWD car also has a centre differential (`center_diff`: open, lsd or locked) and a torque split `center_split` (the share to the front axle). All of it is in the car editor and the tuning menu.

* Without a clutch pedal the car works the clutch itself (it engages with the engine speed and opens while shifting), so it does not stall. With a clutch pedal on a calibrated wheel the clutch is yours: release it too quickly and the engine stalls (clutch + throttle, or **I**, starts it again), dump it at high revs and the tyres spin.
* **The gearbox of the car decides how it is shifted** (the Drivetrain card of the car editor and the tuning menu, `drivetrain.gearbox`; there is no gearbox-mode key any more):
  * *Manual* (`gears`) and *Manual with an automatic clutch* (`imt`): the gears are 1, 2, … with N and R below 1; the shift buttons (**X**/**Z**, paddles) change the gear and the H-pattern shifter selects it. With a manual and a clutch pedal on a calibrated wheel the pedal is used once it is pressed or the shifter is moved (after the paddles the clutch is automatic again); an IMT never uses the pedal, its clutch is automatic.
  * *Automated manual* (`amt`), *Dual clutch* (`dct`), *Automatic with a torque converter* (`torque-converter`) and *CVT* (`cvt`) are automatics with the selector P R N D: the shift buttons move the selector (P and R only while standing still), **.** is reverse while it is held, in P the wheels are locked, and the display shows D and the gear it is in (the CVT shows D). An AMT shifts slowly (half a second at least, `shift_time`) with the power off; the others are described below.
* **The systems of a car** (the *Assists and extras* card of the tuning menu and of the car editor, each with a tick: traction control, ABS, stability control, launch control, the two-step, with their settings under the tick; `extras.tc_on` and so on in `car.json`): a car without one has no light for it on the gauge and its key does nothing. The cards of the car editor are those of the tuning menu, laid out the same way (`CarTuning.CARDS`): a setting is a line with its number and unit, a ↺ back to the value of the car as loaded, and a slider under it, in folding groups; the slider covers the range of the shared table (`CarTuning.GROUPS`, wide: springs 3 to 200 N/mm, camber −12 to 6°, grip 20 to 300 %, power 10 to 500 %, and so on) and the number field takes up to twice the end of it.
* **Traction control** (*C*, on by default) only looks at the wheels: when a driven wheel turns faster than the road under it (it spins), power is taken off the engine until it grips again.
* **Reverse with the brake key (automatic gearbox in D).** Hold the brake key at a standstill (or below a speed that the settings menu sets, 3 km/h by default) and the car goes into reverse while the key is held; in reverse the brake key is the gas and the gas key brakes, and the gas key at a standstill is D again. It is off (the brake key only brakes) with launch control on, and at the start line of a drag strip, where the brake key holds the car. It works for analog pedals too (a wheel, the triggers of a pad, the touch pedals): the brake pedal is the gas of the reverse, the gas pedal the brake. The keys and the pedals have their own switch in the settings menu.
* **Tyre model.** Every car has a tyre that is a body of nodes and beams, which finds the road itself: the nodes (and the places on the beams between them) are tested against every triangle of the track (the road, the faces of kerbs, the sides of ramps, walls), so the tyre does not go through the side of a ramp, and every node in contact holds the surface with a bristle (a spring and a damper, up to the friction of the surface times what presses it, with a fall to the sliding friction), so the grip, the combined slip, the stick at a standstill and the way a tyre lets go come out of the nodes. The sums go to the wheel: the load along the suspension, the forces along the road, and what presses across the suspension (the side of a ramp) to the car at the hub. `tyres.shell` in car.json (*Tyre* in the tuning menu and the car editor) sets the tread: `width` (0: from the wheel), `rings` of nodes across the tread (1 to 5) and `segments`, the nodes round the wheel (4 to 72: a square, a hexagon, ... fewer is cheaper; the road is felt at four places between each two nodes, so a tyre with few sides still rolls round). The tyre is the **shell** (below); the older cylinder body without the air pressure, the ring of nodes and the 2D physics are gone (`tyres.model` in car.json is not read any more). The debug view (0) draws the nodes and beams.
* **The shell tyre** (`TyreShell.js`, the tyre of every car) is two shells: an outer shell of free nodes (the tread, the rings and nodes of `tyres.shell`; a car tyre has a flat crown with a small shoulder, a motorcycle tyre a round one) and an inner shell that follows the rim (`tyres.shell.inner_ratio`, the radius of the inner shell as a share of the tyre, 62% by default). The nodes of the two shells are joined by beams: a cable from every outer node to the rim that only pulls and is taut by the air pressure (`tyres.pressure_front/pressure_rear`: the force on the outer node outwards and the same force back on the rim), belt springs round the tyre and across the tread, shear springs along and across the tread (the sidewall), and a bump stop deep in. The carcass goes slack (the air carries the node) at `tyres.shell.carcass` mm of push-in; 0 takes 1.8 times the static deflection of the tyre, which gives the tyre the stiffness of the car's `tyre_rate` and lets it follow the pressure (a harder tyre goes in less, a softer one has a longer patch). The road presses on the outer nodes and on four places between each two nodes, and the nodes in contact hold the surface with the same bristles as the cylinder tyre. The tyre has **camber**: the wheel leans by its static camber less what the roll of the body takes (`chassis.camber_front/rear`, `roll_camber`), the wheel of the model leans with it, the shoulder on the inside carries more and a leaning tyre pushes to the side it leans to (camber thrust: a twentieth of the cornering stiffness per radian, a car tyre has about a tenth); toe is the steering angle of the wheel. The shell turns the wheel itself in its substeps with the torque of the tread (the car adds the engine and the brake): the grip saturates within a few millimetres of turn, which one step of the car cannot follow. The tyre of the wheel model shows the deformation (`TyreDeform.js`): its tread is pushed in as far as the outer nodes are, by the vertex shader, and the rim does not move; `OpenRacer.TyreDeform.gain = 8` in the console draws it eight times as deep. It costs about 2.3 ms per wheel per step against 0.15 ms for the cylinder. **Known limits:** the rolling resistance grows with the speed (Crr 0.01 at 18 km/h, 0.04 at 54, 0.10 at 108: the lumped tread loses energy going in and out of the patch, and the tread is measured against a round wheel where it is flat), the relaxation length is short (0.09 m against 0.3 to 0.5 m: the sidewall is a shear spring per node, not a belt that moves as one), and the patch is long (26 cm). The next step is a belt with its own stiffness between the nodes and a soft sidewall under it.
* **Turbo with a boost pressure.** In the tuning menu (*Engine*), *Turbo: boost pressure at full boost* (bar; 0 keeps the older model with a share of the torque) makes the boost give power with the physics of the air: the pressure ratio, the heat of the compressed air (compressor efficiency, 70% by default), the intercooler (75%) and the back pressure of the exhaust (3.5% per bar). The torque curve is then that of the engine without the turbo; at 1 bar the engine has about 1.8 times the power, at 2 bar about 2.5 times, at 4 bar about 3.5 times. The boost builds up with the throttle and the spool time, and the boost gauge shows the real pressure.
* **Rev limiter, two-step, transbrake and launch control.** The rev limiter cuts the fuel at the limit, at the rate of *Cuts per second* (tuning menu and car editor). Hold *Q* for the **two-step**: at a standstill the engine is held at its own lower speed, and the automatic clutch opens. With a clutch pedal the two-step can work with the pedal instead: switch it on with *Y* (settings menu) and the pedal in at a standstill holds the engine speed. Hold *F* for the **transbrake**: the driven wheels are held and the clutch is open, so the engine runs free; let go and the car launches. **Launch control** (*J*, off by default): with the brake and full throttle at a standstill the engine is held at the launch speed; let go of the brake and the car launches, with the traction control working for the start (also when it is off). The engine speeds are in the tuning menu.
* **Drag strip.** In the track editor, *Drag strip* opens a window to choose the distance (1/8 mile, 1000 ft, 1/4 mile, 1/2 mile, 1 km, 1 mile or your own length) and the width; click on the road where the start line is and drag in the direction of the strip, and the finish line is placed that far away (saved in track.json as `dragstrip`). In the game, near the start line a text shows how far the front wheels are from it. With the front wheels at the line and the car standing still, "Get ready" shows for two seconds (time for the two-step; the settings menu sets it), and then the lights come: stage, three ambers and green; they go out again a second and a half later. Leaving before the green light is a false start: the run stops, and the car has to go back behind the line and come up slowly again. The times and the speeds at 60 ft, 330 ft, 1/8 mile, 1000 ft, 1/4 mile and so on, as far as the strip goes, are only shown at the end: a time slip slides in from the top at the right of the screen, with the times on it (a cross or Escape closes it).
* **Drag strip top 50.** For a track with a drag strip the main menu has a *Drag strip: Top 50* button. The list has a tab for every distance and shows the best 50 runs of that distance in this browser (the runs are kept while *Save lap records* is on): the car (click it for a scrollable window with the most important tuning and car information of that run), the 1/4 and 2/4 mile times, the top speed, and a *Ticket* button that shows the time slip of that run. In the track editor the Layers panel has an *Events* group: the drag strip can be chosen there to move it (arrows) and turn it (circle).
* **Extras in the tuning menu** (the *Extras* group; each has a checkbox that opens its settings, and they are saved with the car under `extras` in car.json): **Governor** (a soft rev limit at an engine speed of your choice: the throttle closes gently over the last 250 rpm, so the engine holds that speed instead of bouncing on the rev limiter; for a CVT at full throttle at top speed), **Pops and bangs** (unburnt fuel burns in the exhaust: at every cut of the rev limiter and the two-step, and for a time after you lift off the throttle; with a sound, flames at the tail pipes and settings for how often, how loud and how big the flames are), **Anti-lag** (off the throttle, or in a launch hold, the turbo is kept at a share of its boost with fuel burning in the exhaust, so the throttle gives full boost at once; it has a time limit, burns extra fuel, and the engine brakes less) and a **Boost gauge** (the pressure of the manifold, from the vacuum of a closed throttle to the full boost, with a peak mark; for a car with a turbo). The tail pipes are guessed from the car, or placed in the car editor (the *Exhaust* layer: add and remove points, each with a gizmo for the place and one for the direction; a window with the settings of the chosen point: the diameter of the pipe, the smoke after a bang, the length and the width of the flame (1 to 80 cm) and its brightness; a *Test* button shows the flames and the smoke; saved in car.json as `"exhaust": [{ "position": [x, y, z], "rotation": [degrees], "diameter": m, "smoke", "brightness" (factors), "flame_length_cm", "flame_width_cm" }]`). The flames can be your own pictures: flat PNGs with transparency in `web/images/flames/` named `flame1.png`, `flame2.png`, ... (see the README.txt there).
* **ESC**, the electronic stability control (**N**, on by default; the *ESC* light shows when it works): it compares what the driver asks for (the steering angle, which gives the direction that the car should turn to at that speed) with what the car does (its yaw rate and the direction it moves in). When these differ it steps in: a car that turns more than asked (oversteer, a rear end that slides out) gets a short brake on the outer front wheel, a car that turns less (understeer) gets the inner rear wheel braked, and both take power off the engine. The steering itself is never changed. What the steering asks for depends on the car: its understeer gradient is worked out from the weight on each axle and the stiffness of its tyres, so a nose-heavy car is not read as oversteering when it only turns the way it always does. It has a limit, so a sudden steer from left to right at high speed can still make the car slide; with ESC off the car slides when it asks for more than the tyres have. ABS (**B**) is on by default. **Space** is the handbrake, **R** puts the car back at the start.
* **Cameras** (**T** switches; telemetry is on **V**; also in the settings menu): 1 follows the direction of the car with a smooth rotation and has a wider field of view the faster the car goes, while the camera comes closer in the same measure, so that the car keeps its size in the picture and only the sense of speed changes (a dolly zoom; "comes closer" in the editor sets how far this goes), 2 is the cockpit, and 3 the free camera (`FreeCam.js`): it is moved with the keys and the mouse, as the free camera of the replay: **W A S D** move it, **Ctrl** and **Alt** take it up and down, the **mouse** looks around (click the scene; Escape lets the pointer go), **Q** and **E** roll the picture, **3** and **4** make it slower and faster, **[** and **]** narrow and widen its field of view, **P** makes it look at the car, **O** makes it move with the car, **I** fixes it to the car as a part of it (its place and its turn go with the car; moving with the car or fixed to it, it does not go through a wall, as the chase camera); these keys do not drive the car while it is on, the arrows still do and Space is the handbrake; it is remembered per car (place, look, field of view, speed and the toggles), so it is back where it was the next time, also after a restart; the first time it starts 10 m above where the camera was, fixed to the car (I switches that off). For the other cameras **[** and **]** turn the camera and **`** puts it back. The place of every camera is set per car, in the car editor (*Cameras*); without settings a car gets defaults from its size.

* **Skid marks** (`SkidMarks.js`): a tyre that slides past the peak of its grip at some speed leaves a strip of rubber along the path of its contact patch, darker the harder it slides (black on the road, a lighter track on grass and dirt), for the player's car and the AI alike. One mesh holds about a kilometre of marks in a ring; the oldest are drawn over when it is full.
* **Tyre smoke and squeal**: a tyre that slides (past the peak of its grip) at some speed makes smoke, and the longer it slides the hotter it gets and the thicker the smoke (it goes away slowly once it grips again). On the grass and the ground it is dust. The tyres squeal on the road when they get near and past the limit, higher in pitch with the speed and louder with the slide (quieter in the cockpit). The squeal is made by the game (raw tones that wobble, rubber grit and a rumble, not a filtered noise); if there is a recording in `web/sounds/tyre-squeal.ogg` (or `.mp3`/`.wav`, see `web/sounds/README.md`) that is played instead, with a pitch and volume that follow the car. It comes from the physics, so it also shows with locked wheels under braking (ABS off), wheel spin and drifts; `web/js/Smoke.js`.

* The car collides with walls, fences, buildings and steep embankments (`web/js/Collision.js`): the steep triangles of the track are kept as outlines in a grid, and the car is a rectangle that is pushed out of them. A hit gives an impulse at the point that hit, so it stops the car, makes it bounce a little and spins it when it hits with a corner. Things lower than 30 cm (kerbs) are driven over.

## 3D contact and collisions
The road used to be a height field (`GroundWorld.query(x, z)`: one height per place, the walls as outlines on the ground plane), which cannot hold a loop, a road that goes up a wall or over a bridge. The physics is 3D (the old 2D physics is gone; a track that still says `"physics": "2d"` runs with the 3D one):

* **The world.** Every triangle of the track (the drivable ones for the wheels, the rest that the car can hit) is kept in a 3D grid (`Ground.js`: `addTriangle3D`, `ray`, `nearby`, `closest`, `lineRange`).
* **The wheels** feel the road with rays along the axis of their suspension (five per wheel, ahead of and behind the middle, so a kerb lifts the wheel gradually), whichever way that axis points in the world. The car can be on a wall, or upside down, as long as the road is under its wheels. Gravity stays down in the world.
* **The body** is a cloud of spheres in the frame of the car (`collisionSpheres`), tested against the triangles. A contact with a surface that the car could be riding on (up in the world, or up for the car) is a damped spring with friction (`bodyFriction`, 0.3), the underside that scrapes, the body after a landing. Any other surface is a wall: if the car goes into it, it is pushed out in steps of at most 15 cm and gets an impulse; a wall that is lower than the kerb height (30 cm) above the underside is driven over, measured along the car (the top of the wall above the point of contact, `lineRange`); a contact with the edge or the corner of a triangle that does not point as the face does is the seam between two triangles and is left to the neighbour.
* **The collision shape is made from the model of the car** when it has loaded (`CollisionProxy.js`): the surface of the model is sampled, the car is cut into columns seen from above, and every column gets a sphere at its lowest point, one at its highest and, at the edge, one in between (at most about 90), and a smaller sphere at every wheel. A car without a model that fits gets a shape made from its size. A long nose, a spoiler or a high roof hit things where the model does.
* **Tested** with the loop of a test track made in Blender: a car at 45 m/s or more goes up the wall, over the top upside down and lands on the exit; on the circuit, the skidpad and the walls the 3D and the standard physics give the same results (the trajectory, 1.06 g, the impact on the wall at the same place), at the same frame rate with eight cars. The physics bench has no track and is unchanged.

## The roadmap of the physics
1. **3D contact and collisions** (done, see above).
2. **The collision shape from the model** (done: `CollisionProxy.js`).
3. **A car that deforms** (done in 2.2.0, `SoftBody.js`, see [SOFTBODY.md](SOFTBODY.md)): a shell of nodes and beams in the frame of the car, made from the model. It dents and stays bent, parts hang on hinges and come loose, wheels bend or come off, and two cars dent each other. The rigid body stays the frame of the car, so the driving does not change; a hit adds the deformation to it. *Damage* in the main menu (`&damage=0`) switches it off.

## Physics bench
`node tools/physics-bench.js` runs the physics of every car (Vehicle.js, Engine.js, Wheel.js, without the browser) on a flat road and measures what a road test would: 0–100 km/h and 0–60 mph (traction control off and on), the braking distance from 100 km/h and from 60 mph (ABS on), the top speed, the most lateral acceleration the car holds on a skidpad (a fixed steering angle, the speed raised slowly until the car does not follow any more), the understeer gradient at 60 and 120 km/h (the steering past what the kinematics need, in degrees per g) and the share of the load transfer that the front axle takes. The bench has reference figures of a real car only for the Mustang Mach 1 (0–60 mph about 6.5 s, 60–0 mph about 40 m, about 0.8 g, about 190 km/h), which is no longer in the game, so the cars of today are measured without a comparison. `node tools/physics-bench.js <id>` runs one car, `--json` prints the figures. Run it after a change to the physics or to a car: the tyre's load sensitivity (`Wheel.loadSensitivity`, a fifth of the grip lost when the load doubles) and the load transfer were set with it.

Next to it, `web/tools/physics-check.html` (open it from the server) runs the physics inside the game for the cars you pick: the ride height, a landing from 3 m and from 10 m, the wheels on a straight at 30 m/s (a wheel that flickers) and a bend (the yaw against the steering), and marks what is outside the limits.

**Look and atmosphere:** the Graphics page of the settings menu has the tone mapping, the exposure, the contrast and the saturation (against an overblown picture), *anisotropic filtering* and *sharpening*, and an atmosphere that is the same on every track: the *time of day* (dawn to night, with an hour slider), the direction of the sun, the phase of the moon, the *weather* (clear, cloudy, fog, rain, snow) and the *ground*. Rain wets the road and snow lies on it and stays; both give less grip (snow most) for you and for the AI, which drives slower. Tracks of the old format with a sky in their model keep their own sky.

**Performance:** the graphics settings have a *draw distance* (500 to 5000 m; the presets set it) and AI cars more than 450 m from the camera are not drawn. **F2** saves the picture of the game as a PNG.

## Tyre bench
`node tools/tyre-bench.js [car] [--quick] [--tests roll,slipx,...] [--set=parameter=value] [--verbose] [--json]` runs the tyre alone (TyreShell.js, without the car) on a flat road with a hub that is held where the bench wants it, as a tyre test rig would, and holds every figure against the range of a real passenger car tyre; what is outside is marked CHECK. The tyre is set up from the car's car.json (radius, pressure, stiffness, rings and nodes, unsprung mass, `tyre_rate`). Pass the id of a car: the default, `openracer-mustang`, is no longer in the game. It measures the static curve (deflection, stiffness, patch, at the nominal pressure and at 0.6 and 1.4 times it), the free roll (rolling radius, rolling resistance at 5, 15 and 30 m/s, the pressure centre, the noise of the load and the friction), the slip ratio sweep (slip stiffness, peak, where it is, what is left at 100 %, drive against brake), the slip angle sweep (cornering stiffness, peak, where it is), the camber sweep (camber stiffness against the cornering stiffness, and the direction), combined slip (the friction circle), the relaxation length, a wheel mass of 20, 35 and 90 kg on the tyre after a kick (the swing must not grow), the wheel spinning against the tread at a standstill after a torque pulse (the swing must die), the substeps and the cost. `--set=beamDamping=0.3` (any parameter of the tyre body) tries a value; `--tests` picks tests (the static curve is always run). Run it after a change to a tyre model, before the change is felt in the game.

## Rigid objects in a track
Things that can be knocked over (barrels, boxes, cones, balls) lie asleep in the track and are only simulated when a car touches them; after a while without moving they sleep again (`web/js/RigidObjects.js`, the engine is Rapier). Two ways to put them in a track:

* in the model of the track: name a part, or a node above it, `rigid_...` (`rigid_barrel_1`, `Rigid Cone.002`). It becomes a body of the shape of the part (convex hull) with a mass from its box (250 kg/m³, `"rigid_density"` in track.json) or from the name (`rigid_barrel_60kg`). It is no longer part of the road or the walls.
* in `track.json`: `"objects": [{ "shape": "box", "x": 10, "z": 20, "size": [1, 0.8, 1], "mass": 40, "grid": [1, 5], "layers": 3, "heading": 90, "color": "#c9a25b" }, { "shape": "cylinder", "x": 12, "z": 22, "radius": 0.3, "height": 0.9 }]`. Shapes: `box`, `cylinder`, `cone`, `sphere`; `y` is the road when left out; `grid` and `spacing` make a block, `layers` a stack. A model placed in the track editor has a kind: *rigid* (a body that is pushed over, `mass` in kg or from its size), *static* (`"kind": "static"`: it stands still and can be hit, its triangles join the walls and the 3D grid as scenery) or *decor* (`"kind": "decor"`, also written as `"rigid": false`: only drawn, no physics knows it, so it costs nothing in the game).

* in the track editor: the *Objects* card with the library of models (small pictures, the same in every track, in the folder `objects/`): click a model to place it, Escape goes back to Look; **+ Add a model** (a glb; its origin is put at the middle of its base, or keep the origin of the file with the select), then *Place object*, press on the road and drag to say which way it faces; choose a placed object in the Layers panel (or click it in the view) and use **W** (move), **E** (turn) and **R** (scale) on its gizmo, Delete takes it away (`pivot`, `pitch`, `roll` and `scale` of the object in track.json); tick *rigid* (and a mass in kg) for a body that is pushed, or leave it off for a model that stays.

A car pushes them and feels their mass: the change of speed of the objects it hits is taken from its own speed.

## Skidpad
*Skidpad*, a way to play in the main menu (after *Play*; then the car and the ground: asphalt, wet asphalt, grass, gravel, sand or mud, `&surface=`), takes the chosen car to `web/tracks/skidpad` (a round pad with a painted circle of 50 m radius, made by `tools/make-skidpad.py`) and drives it round the circle by itself (`?mode=skidpad`, `startSkidpad` in `main.js`): the steering holds the painted line, the speed goes up a quarter of a metre per second every second, and the lateral acceleration is read from the tyre forces while the car still follows the circle. When it lets go (off the line or sliding) the result comes up: the g it held, the speed, and *Reset* (again, with the tuning as it is now), *Tune* (the tuning menu of the game, so you can change the car and press Reset) and *Back to the menu*. The ESC is off for it; the traction control follows your setting. The same test as the bench's skidpad, but to look at.

## Racing: AI opponents, the ghost and lap records
*Play* in the main menu goes through steps: **Free drive**, **Race** or **Skidpad**, then the car (and its setup, when it has one), the track, the weather, and for a race the **laps** (3, 5, 10 or 25), the **opponents** (1, 2, 3, 5 or 7), their **difficulty** and damage, with *More options…* for the **ghost** of your best lap, whether **lap records** are saved, tyre wear and the cars that may race, and last the opponents themselves. All of it is remembered; it goes in the address of the game (`&laps=5&ai=3&difficulty=normal&ghost=1&records=1`).

* **A race** starts standing: everyone is held on the grid (the brakes are on, the engine can be revved) through a 3-2-1-GO countdown; the clocks of the race run from GO. The HUD shows the lap of the race (LAP 2/5, FINAL LAP) and the place. An AI car that has done its laps slows down and pulls to the right, out of the way. When you cross the line after your last lap the results come up: the place, the best lap and the time of every car (the gap to the winner; a car still on its way is *running* until it comes in), with *Race again* and *Main menu*. Practice has no countdown and no end.

* **AI cars** (`web/js/Opponent.js`) have the same physics and models as the player (the other cars of the game, in other colours) and are driven by a driver that follows the racing line: it steers at a point ahead on the line (further the faster) with a pull towards the line when it is beside it, keeps to the speed the line allows there (from the curvature, with braking for the bends ahead), moves aside for a car in front and backs off when it is right behind one; the assists are on and the gearbox shifts by itself. The difficulty is how close to the limit of the tyres it drives. A crash (a hard hit: the speed over the road lost 8 m/s in a fifth of a second; the car on its side or its roof for a second; a wheel torn off) stops the car where it came to rest for three seconds, and then it is put back, repaired, at the right side of the track a little further along the line, standing and facing along it; a car that is stuck for seven seconds or far off the line goes the same way, one that fell out of the world at once. A car with a live axle (the parts library) leans its wheels with the axle, as the player's does. An AI car drives on a simple tyre (`Vehicle.simpleTyres`, `Wheel.tyreForce`): rays along the suspension find the road and a slip curve gives the grip, where the player's tyres are shells of nodes (TyreShell.js) that cost most of the time of a car; with the shells, every AI car cost as much as the player and the frame rate fell with each car on the grid (one car took it from 115 to 6 frames a second; three now leave it at about 70). The cars bump into each other. They start on the grid ahead of the player; the HUD shows the place (P2/4) from how far along the line everyone is.
* **Tyre wear and fuel** (`Vehicle.js`, `Pit.js`; the option *Tyre wear and fuel* of the main menu, in the track step of free drive and under *More options…* of a race: off, on, or fast for a short race, `&wear=0|1|3`). A tyre has a working window (about 70–100 °C): cold it grips less, overheated too; it warms from sliding and from flexing under its load, and cools with the air that passes. It wears with the sliding, faster when it is overheated, and a worn tyre grips less (a tyre that is gone a lot less). The fuel burns with the power of the engine (the tank, 60 L by default, and the use are in `car.json` under `fuel` and in the tuning menu, with the heating and the life of the tyres under *Tyres*); the car gets lighter as it goes, and an empty tank stops the engine. The HUD shows the four tyres (the colour is the temperature, the dark part the wear), the fuel and a PIT hint. **A pit stop** is made by standing still on the pit lane (the surface *Pit lane* of the track editor, `track01`): the tank is filled at 6 L/s and, when the tyres are worn past 15 %, new tyres go on, which takes longer the more worn they are (2.5–7.5 s), with a moment to lift and drop the car; the two happen side by side, so the stop takes as long as what is needed. The HUD counts it down. The AI pits too when its tyres are worn or its fuel will not last the laps that are left, driving the pit lane at a walking pace: the line through the lane is found from the pit lane surface and the racing line. A track without a pit lane has no stops.
* **The racing line** (`RacingLine.js`) is the best lap that was driven on the track in this browser (its ghost; `&bestline=0` skips it), else `racing_line` in `track.json` when the track has one, else the middle of the road of a designed track: on a track from Blender, drive one lap and the AI can race from the next start. A line from a lap is smoothed (a moving average over 10 m, four times, round the loop): a driven lap wobbles, and where it is closed the end does not meet the start, since the car crosses the timing line somewhere else a lap later; unsmoothed, that joint was a kink the speed profile read as a hairpin, and every AI car braked to a crawl just past the start. The speed of the line comes from the bend (1.05 g for a driver of full skill), scaled by the grip of the surface under the line (sand or grass: the share of the road's grip, from `Helpers.getSurface`), and held at the crest of a dune to 15 % over the speed at which the car leaves the ground there (from the second derivative of the height of the line over 6 m), so the AI hops rather than flies off the track; it is never below 6 m/s. The track editor records the line: *AI racing line → Record AI racing line* opens the game with the track, you drive a calm lap through all the gates (every lap you finish replaces the line) and Esc or the Stop button saves the last lap as the line and brings you back to the editor (with `start-server.sh`); *Forget the line* removes it. In the game, *Esc → Tools → Racing line of this track* saves your best lap as the line. The line runs in the direction of the start.
* **Choosing the opponents** (the last step of a race): a grid of four places in a row, as many as *Opponents* says. A + opens the choice of a car and, when it has a saved tune, of the tune; the copy button on a car adds the same one again, ✕ removes it, and *Start* races against exactly these cars (each with its tune). Nothing chosen: the places get cars of the game.
* **Damage on or off** (a row of every way to play in the main menu: with the tyre wear in free drive, in the set-up of a race, and under *More options*; `&damage=0|1`; on by default, kept between games). Off, every car is a plain rigid body: no shell that dents (SoftBody.js), no parts or wheels that come off, no punctures (the *Tyre damage* tick of the tuning menu does nothing then); the spheres of the body still touch the road and the walls, so a car still lands on its roof and bumps into the others. Cheaper too: the shell is a few hundred nodes per car.
* **The ghost** (`Ghost.js`) is your best lap with this car on this track, driven again as a see-through car while you drive; the lap that is going is recorded (thirty times a second) and kept when it is the best. **Lap records** (`Records.js`) are kept per track and car in the browser (localStorage) and shown in the main menu and the HUD. *Esc → Tools → Records of this track → Forget them* clears them.

## Replay
Everything is recorded from the first frame of the game (`web/js/Replay.js`): all cars (the player and the opponents) thirty times a second, as what is needed to draw them again (the body, the speed, the steering and for every wheel its place, spin and spring), in blocks of `Float32Array` (about 4 KB a second for a car; the last half hour is kept). **Esc → Replay** stops the game where it is and plays the recording through `updateVisuals` of the cars, so the suspension rig, the steering wheel and the cameras work as in the game. The bar at the bottom has a slider over the whole recording, rewind (backwards), pause, play, jumps of ten seconds and the speed 0.1x, 0.25x, 0.5x, 1x, 2x, 3x and 5x. Enter pauses and plays, the arrows jump five seconds, Home and End go to the ends; Esc or *Back to the game* returns to the game with the cars put back as they were. Skid marks and smoke are not recorded.

**Zoom to the mouse** in both editors: the wheel zooms towards the point under the mouse (a ray to the model or the track, else the floor), and the orbit centre moves there in the same measure, so the view turns about what was zoomed in on, as in Blender. A scroll beside every surface can send the camera of the car editor far away: the reset button at the top right of the view (next to the perspective switch; also the Home key) puts the camera back at its standard place, looking at the car, in perspective.

**The pictures of the tracks** in the menu: the track editor has a *Thumbnail* button that takes one from the camera as it stands (the track as the game shows it, without the nodes, the markers and the gizmos; 640 by 360), saved with the track as `preview.webp`; a track without one gets a picture made in the menu from behind its start, kept in the browser and written to the track when the server may.

**A take** (`Take.js`): the **5** key starts one (a red dot with the time at the top of the HUD), **5** again stops it, and a window asks what it should be: a *Replay* (that part of the recording goes into the replay library of the main menu, to watch or download) or a *Video* (a second question: 16:9, 9:16 or 1:1; the replay opens on that part, plays and films it as the Video button of the replay does, with the camera as it was during the take, the free camera where it was put included, saves the file and closes again, and the game goes on); the cross or Cancel throws the take away.

The cameras of the replay: Follow and Cockpit are those of the game, and **Free** is a camera that is moved by hand (`Replay.js`, *the free camera*): WASD or the arrows move it level along where it looks, Shift and Control go up and down, the mouse (click the scene: pointer lock, Esc lets go) or the right stick looks around, `[` and `]` change the field of view, **F** aims it at the car (the look rests), **L** fixes it to the car (its place and look are then kept in the frame of the car; it starts that way, so it does not stay behind when the car drives on) and Tab chooses the car. Every movement, the look and the zoom are smoothed (the velocity follows what is asked for, the look and the field of view follow their targets exponentially), and a slider from 0.01 to 3 scales the speed of all of it (kept in `localStorage`). H hides the bar.

**● Video** records the replay as a video (a `MediaRecorder` on the canvas: mp4 where the browser can, else webm). In the game: a .glb dropped on the car editor becomes a car (the wheels, the size and the floor are found by themselves; *Save and test*), `Esc → Ghost` saves the best lap as a `.openracer-ghost` file and races the lap of a file that someone sent (`Ghost.js`), and a jump of more than 0.6 s shows its distance, time and height (`JumpStats.js`).

**Cinematic** is a camera that uses the cinematic cameras of the track (`cameras` in track.json, put there with *Add camera* in the track editor): the one whose range (a circle on the floor, `range` with `x`, `z`, `r` in a camera) the car is in, else the nearest, which stays where it is and turns to follow the car, with a field of view that keeps the car about the same size; it goes to another camera when that one is at least 15% nearer.

A replay can be saved and shared: **Save replay** in the bar keeps it in **Replays** of the main menu (the browser's IndexedDB: the stores `library` for the list and `blobs` for the bytes), and the game asks whether to save the recording before it goes back to the main menu after a race or practice (`Replay.confirmLeave`). **Replays** is a window with the saved ones (Watch, Download, Delete) and **Import replay…**; a download is a `.openracer-replay` file (a small header with the ids of the track and the cars and the version of the game, then the numbers as `Float32`, compressed with gzip when the browser can) and a file is read when it is imported or watched: it is checked (format, version, sizes, every number finite, the ids of the track and the cars against this game, with a message that names what is missing), kept in IndexedDB, and the game is started with `&replay=1` to show it without a game behind it (`setupReplayViewer` in `main.js`; Esc goes to the main menu). The file has the recording, not the models: the other one needs the same cars and track, as standard ones or as zips of the garage. About 14 KB a second for four cars.

## HUD
Bottom right is a round gauge: the ring is the engine speed (with a red zone at the rev limit and a shift light that goes orange and flashes at the limiter), in the middle are the speed and the gear (P R N D and the gear of the automatic, or 1, 2, … N R), and the two small arcs are the throttle (green, left) and the brake (red, right). Under the gear stand LAUNCH and 2-STEP, one above the other, and along the bottom of the gauge, on an arc, the badges of traction control, ESP and ABS (`web/images/HUD`; only the systems the car has): orange when the system is on, grey when it is switched off (TC has its own OFF badge), and blinking while the system is at work; a click or a tap on a badge switches the system, as the keys do. ENGINE OFF shows under the gear while the engine is off. A red P shows while the parking lock holds the car and TB while the transbrake holds. A system that is not fitted (the tuning menu, *Systems*) has no badge and no key. The boost gauge of a turbo car sits at the top left of the gauge. Top left are the lap, the time of the lap that is going and the last laps (the best one is green).

In the settings menu (**Esc**) the HUD can be switched off (**H** by default, a key that can be remapped), the units are metric (km/h) or imperial (mph), and *Debug text* (**F3**) shows the technical text at the top of the screen (wheel values, clutch, FPS).

## Settings and controls
Press **Esc** for the settings menu (the game pauses). It has options for traction control, ABS, the gearbox (automatic or manual) and the engine sound, and the list of all actions with their inputs. Every action can have several inputs at once: a key, a button or axis of a controller and a button of a racing wheel, shown as small chips. **✎** sets a new input (press a key or button, or move an axis; Esc cancels), **✕** removes it and **+** adds one. Buttons, axes and the D-pad of a wheel or controller can all be used (a D-pad that the browser reports as one axis with a value per direction is recognised, and every direction can have its own action). An input belongs to one action: taking it for another action removes it from the first. The pedals, steering and H-shifter of a wheel are measured by the wheel calibration, which can be redone per input from the same list. The bindings are stored in the browser (localStorage).

## three.js
The game uses three.js r186 (`web/js/three`, vendored: the build and the few add-ons for post-processing). three.js is an ES module, so `web/js/bootstrap.js` imports it, exposes it as the global `THREE` and then loads the other scripts in order. The old JSON car models are converted to `BufferGeometry` by `parseLegacyModel` in `Helpers.js`.

## A driver in the car
The *Driver* card of the car editor loads a driver (a .glb with a simple skeleton in the T pose, from Blender) and saves it with the car (`driver` in car.json). Place the model, the two grips of the hands on the steering wheel and the two feet on the pedals with the gizmos (inverse kinematics bends the arms and the legs; the bones are found by their names and can be set by hand), and try it with the sliders for the steering, the throttle and the brake. In the game the hands hold the steering wheel and pass over the way a driver does (at 130 degrees the trailing hand lets go and goes to the top in one slow movement by the chest, out of the way of the other arm, the leading hand rides on to 4 o'clock, then the free hand takes the top and the other lets go and takes it after it), the feet follow the pedals, and the head is hidden in the cockpit view. `web/examples/driver-t-pose.glb` is a test driver to try it with: a figure of blocks with a skeleton in the T pose (Hips, Spine, Chest, Neck, Head, and for each side Shoulder, UpperArm, ForeArm, Hand, Thigh, Shin and Foot, 1.8 m tall: scale it to about 0.6 for a kart).

## Keyboard
**0** switches the debug view on and off: the triangles of the track near the car (green: road, orange: walls, blue-grey: the rest that the body can hit), the collision spheres of the car (white, red where a node is bent or torn off), the nodes and beams of the shell (yellow), the contact point and normal of every wheel (cyan) and, with the tyre model of nodes, the ring of tread nodes of every wheel (green in the patch, red where it slides). It is drawn over everything and costs nothing when it is off.

The steering keys turn the wheels at a rate: fast at a standstill, slower the faster the car goes, and the wheels come back to the middle by themselves when the keys are let go. *Esc → Keyboard → Steering speed* scales that rate (50–300 %).

## Touch screen
On a phone or tablet (a coarse pointer, or `?touch=1` in the address; `?touch=0` switches it off) `web/js/TouchControls.js` draws the controls over the game: the left half of the screen steers with a floating stick under the finger (it follows a finger that goes far), **GAS** and **BRAKE** are pedals on the right that build up smoothly, and buttons give the handbrake, shift up and down, reverse (held), back to the start, the camera and the settings menu (**☰**). Each finger is its own pointer, so one can steer while another brakes. The steering and the pedals go through the same path as a gamepad (`gamepadUpdate` in `controls.js`), so the steering is slower with the speed and has the steering assist; the buttons are actions of the settings (`Settings.setVirtual`). On a touch device the picture is limited to about two million pixels and the anti-aliasing is 2x (`pickPixelRatio` in `Scene.js`); the desktop is not changed.

**A phone** (`TextureBudget.js`): the textures of the models are drawn down to 1024 by 1024 before they go to the graphics card (a car with 4096 by 4096 textures is hundreds of MB on the card with its mipmaps: Safari on an iPhone drew black textures and ended the page), the graphics start on the *Low* preset the first time, the picture is held to about two million pixels, and the HUD (the lap time, the tyres, the gauge, the pills) and the settings menu are smaller on a narrow screen; the debug text at the top is not shown on a touch screen.

## Gamepad
A gamepad steers with the left stick and drives with the triggers (the bindings are in the settings menu). The stick is the steering one to one at a standstill; the faster the car goes, the slower the steering follows the stick, as with the keyboard, so a flick at speed is a small movement of the wheels. *Esc → Gamepad* has a slider for how much slower (off: one to one at every speed) and the check box *Steering assist*: when the rear steps out, the front tyres steer into the slide by themselves (the way a car with a lot of caster does), so it does not become a spin.

## Racing wheel support
Wheels with pedals and a shifter are supported through the browser's Gamepad API.

1. Connect the wheel and press a button on it, so the browser exposes it.
2. Open the settings menu (**Esc**) and press *Calibrate the wheel from scratch*, or use the **K** key. Follow the on-screen steps: steering left/right, throttle, brake, and optionally clutch, shift up/down buttons and an H-pattern shifter (gears and reverse). Every optional step can be skipped with **N**; **Esc** cancels and keeps the previous mapping.
3. The mapping is stored per device in the browser's localStorage, so it survives a refresh (steering, throttle and brake are already kept when the rest of the calibration is not finished). If the browser shows the wheel under another name, the calibration of the last calibrated wheel with the same number of axes and buttons is used. The settings menu shows what is known about the wheel. In the settings menu the ✎ next to a wheel input (pedal, steering, shift button or gear) measures just that input again.
4. **M** switches the wheel off (keyboard only) and on again. The debug line at the top shows the live wheel values.

The wheel steers the car over its whole calibrated range (a 900 degree wheel gives full lock at 450 degrees to each side). *Wheel steering range* in the settings menu lowers this to 75% or 50% of the range for full lock. Around the middle the wheel counts as centred: *Wheel dead zone* sets how much (0 to 5% of the range, 1% by default, about 4 degrees of a 900 degree wheel); the steering starts from zero at the edge of it.

With both an H-pattern shifter and paddles mapped, the most recent action decides: moving the shifter into a gear gives control to the shifter, pulling a paddle gives control to the paddles.

What the shift buttons and the H-pattern shifter of the wheel do depends on the gearbox of the car (see above): the paddles shift in every mode and the H-pattern shifter selects the gears of a manual or an IMT.

The pedals must be reported by the same device as the wheel, and each pedal needs its own axis.

## Force feedback
A racing wheel with force feedback gets the torque that the driver would feel, calculated from the physics every frame (`web/js/ForceFeedback.js`): the aligning torque of the front tyres (their lateral force on the trail of the steering, which collapses as they slide, so the wheel goes light in understeer), the bumps of the road (the speed of the front springs), the rumble of grass and gravel, the judder of sliding front tyres, the pulses of the ABS, the jolt of a hit and a little shake of the engine at idle. It reaches the wheel in one of three ways:

* **HID PID** (the standard for force feedback devices: PXN, Thrustmaster, Fanatec, Moza, Simagic, the Logitech G920/G923 in their native mode and most others) through WebHID: a constant force effect that is updated up to 120 times a second, with a centring spring and a damper that the wheel does itself. The reports are read from the descriptor of the wheel, so no list of wheels is needed.
* **Logitech classic protocol** (G25, G27, G29, Driving Force GT/Pro) through WebHID: the constant force of its first slot and its own centring spring. A G29 has to be in its PC (native) mode.
* **Rumble** through the Gamepad API for a gamepad, or a wheel without the above: the bumps and the slip shake it; there is no torque.

WebHID exists in Chrome and Edge. The wheel is chosen once with *Wheel with force feedback → Connect a wheel…* in the settings menu (the browser shows a window with the devices; take the entry of the wheel itself); after that the game opens it again by itself. The settings menu also has *Force feedback* on/off, *Strength*, *Centring spring*, *Damping*, *Road detail* (bumps, grass, slip, ABS), *Direction* (inverted if the wheel pulls the wrong way) and *Test* (shakes the wheel for two seconds). A PXN wheel has to be in its DirectInput mode (in XInput mode it only centres itself). The debug text shows what is connected and why not.

## Blender: render settings that look like the game
The game and Blender light a car differently, so a car never looks exactly the same, but it can come close. What matters:

* **Materials:** use the Principled BSDF with the inputs that glTF has (base colour, metallic, roughness, normal, emission, alpha, clear coat, transmission). Other shaders (many glass shaders) do not survive the export and arrive as opaque white metal; set them right in the car editor (*Materials*), or use a Principled BSDF with *Transmission* and a low roughness for glass.
* **Colour management** (Render properties): the game uses the tone mapping of the Graphics page of the settings menu (Neutral by default; AgX, ACES or Off). Use the same in Blender: view transform *Standard* with the Tone mapping of the game off, or *AgX* with AgX. Look *None*, gamma 1. The game brightens the picture by 20% at the end (x1.2, which is +0.26 exposure in Blender).
* **Lighting:** the car is lit by the surroundings of the track (the sky and the ground are reflected), by three lights and by a bit of ambient light. Save the surroundings in the game (**Esc**, *Surroundings for Blender*) as `openracer-environment.hdr`, and use it as the world (Environment Texture, strength 1). Add the lights: a sun of strength 2.2 from (350, -250, 75), a sun of strength 1.6 from (40, -40, 70) that casts the shadow and one of strength 3.1 from (500, 0, 1000) (positions in Blender axes; these are the lights of the game).
* **Render engine:** EEVEE, without screen space reflections / ray tracing (the game reflects only the surroundings), without bloom and ambient occlusion; soft shadows; 64 samples. The game has a 35 degree vertical field of view on the fixed camera (a lens of 38 mm on a 24 mm high sensor), and a slight vignette that is left out.
* **The car itself:** y up, forward along +Z in the game (Blender exports a car that looks towards -Y correctly), the origin where you like (the car editor moves it), the wheels as separate parts with their own names.

`tools/blender_match_game.py` does all this: in Blender open the Scripting workspace, open the file, set `HDRI` to the saved `.hdr` (or put it next to the script or the `.blend`) and press Run Script (or `blender car.blend --python tools/blender_match_game.py`). It does not delete anything, and adds a road under the car for the shadow and a camera if there is none. It was tested with Blender 5.1; the settings that do not exist in a version are skipped.

## Engine sound
The engine sound is calculated from the combustion in the cylinders: a port to JavaScript of the parts of [engine-sim](https://github.com/ange-yaghi/engine-sim) (Ange Yaghi, MIT) that make its sound, reduced so that it runs in the browser as an AudioWorklet (`web/js/EngineSimWorklet.js`, `EngineSim.js`, `EngineSound.js`). The gas in every cylinder is simulated: its volume follows the crank (bore, stroke, rod, compression ratio), the valves open with the lobes of the camshaft (duration, lift, lobe centres) with the compressible flow of a port rated in cfm, the mixture from the manifold (whose pressure follows the throttle) is lit by the spark (a timing curve) and burns with an efficiency that differs a little every cycle, heat goes to the walls, and the exhaust runners and the collector are volumes of gas too. The sound of a cylinder is the pressure in its runner above the atmosphere, delayed by the length of the pipes; the cylinders of an exhaust system are added (the layout says which cylinder is on which bank: a crossplane V8 burbles because two cylinders of one bank fire after each other), then engine-sim's synthesizer follows (jitter, the DC taken off, a mix of the signal and its derivative modulated by air noise, a leveler) and the result is convolved with the recorded impulse response of an exhaust (`web/sounds/ir`, from engine-sim; chosen per car). The ignition cut of the rev limiter leaves unburnt mixture that bangs in the hot exhaust. It fades out when the engine stops and is duller in the cockpit view.

The sound of a car is `sound.engine_sim` in its `car.json` (every parameter is explained at the top of `EngineSim.js`); the car editor has presets (inline 4 to V12, boxers), every parameter and a live preview (Play in the view, with the engine speed and the throttle), and the tuning menu changes the main ones while you drive. A V8 costs about a fifth of a processor core at the default simulation rate of 10 kHz; lower *simulation* if the sound stutters. The sound starts at the first key press or click (browsers want that); it is switched on and off, and its volume set, in the settings menu.

## Tuning menu
**U** (a key that can be remapped, also a button in the settings menu) shows or hides the tuning menu, with which the car is changed while it drives: mass, weight distribution, height of the centre of gravity, roll stiffness, springs, dampers, roll centres and camber, grip and stiffness of the tyres, their heating and life, rolling resistance, engine power, idle and rev limit, inertia and engine braking, a turbo (the share of the torque that is boost, the spool time that is its lag, and from which speed it comes), the tank and the fuel use, drive (rear, front or all wheels), final drive, shift time, every gear, reverse, clutch, differentials, brakes, drag, downforce per axle, steering lock and Ackermann, the traction control and ESC, and the engine sound. The power, the weight and the top speed that follow are shown at the top. The tuning of a car is kept in the browser; the menu writes car.json only when *Save* is clicked with *Default* chosen (the car editor writes it too: *Default* in the setups is car.json, the car as made). **Setups** (tunes): a line of the tuning menu, and in the car editor the bar under the title, keeps the car under a name. In the editor the bar has the list of tunes (*Default* is car.json), a Save button that writes to what the list says (*Save the car* writes car.json; *Save tune* overwrites the tune that is chosen, car.json is not touched), *+ New tune* (a name is asked) and ✕ to delete the tune; choosing a tune fills the whole form, the rims and the tyres of the wheel library included, and while the bench runs the car is built again from it where it is, a metre up, the drive going on. A tune is shared as a file: *Export…* in the tuning menu (and *Export* in the bar of the car editor) writes the tune that is chosen, or the car as it is now, as `<car>-<name>.openracer-tune.json` (the configuration without the model), and *Import…* reads such a file of someone else: it becomes a tune of the car it is imported on and is chosen (a tune made for another car is laid over this one; mind the wheels and the parts). **Tunes that come with a car**: tune files next to its car.json, listed in it (`"tunes": ["race.openracer-tune.json"]`), are read with the car (`Catalog.js`, `OpenRacer.Setups.ship`) and are in every list for everyone who has the car, marked *with the car*; *Save* keeps a copy in the browser, which goes before the tune of the car, and *Delete* (*Undo changes* in the tuning menu) takes only that copy away. In the car editor, *Tune file → Ship with the car* writes the chosen tune next to car.json and adds it to the list (only the list is added to car.json as it was saved), and *Take off the car* removes the file and its name; an exported tune file in the folder of the car with its name in `tunes` works the same. In the tuning menu the line has a list with *Default* (the car as made), *Save* (the setup that is chosen is overwritten with the car as it is now; with *Default* chosen it overwrites car.json with the car as it is tuned, after a question, through the API of `tools/serve.py` from this computer: the site has no such API, and a car of the garage or a folder is changed in the car editor), *Save as new…* (a name is asked) and *Delete*. A setup is the whole configuration of the car, everything car.json has but the model: from the editor the car as the form has it (the chassis, the tyres, the engine, the gearbox, the suspension and the parts, the driver, the soft body, the cameras, the sound, the colours), from the tuning menu the car as it is tuned; they are kept in this browser (`openracer.setups.<id>`). As soon as a car has a setup, the main menu gets a step of its own after the car, **Choose your tune**: a tile for *Default* (the car as made) and one for every setup, each with its own picture, drawn like the picture of the car (the model with the rims, the tyres, the parts and the colours of the tune, on a transparent background; kept in this browser and drawn again when the tune changes), its weight, drive and power; the tune chosen is laid over car.json when the game starts (the model stays the car's own), so everything of it applies, and the bar at the bottom names it. **The colours of the car** are in the Chassis group of the tuning menu: a colour picker for the body (every mesh of the model called body, by its own name, a parent's or its material's) and one for the rims (every mesh called rim: a wheel part of the model, a rim of the wheel library), with ↺ back to the colours of the model; they go with the setup (`colors: { body, rim }` in the configuration). In the tuning menu, loading a setup takes the values of the table from it (the rest, the suspension and the parts, comes at the next start), and the list shows the setup in use, with *(changed)* once the tuning has moved away from it; in the editor, loading one fills the whole form. **The rest of the car editor is in the menu too**, everything but the model and the driver: the suspension type of each axle and the engine placement of the parts library, the efficiency of the driveline and the lock-up clutch, the size of the car and the steering wheel's lock to lock, a torque curve from a peak power and its rpm (a change makes a new curve; a curve drawn by hand in the editor stays until then), the cameras, the soft body (on or off, its nodes, the strength of the wheels), the rider and the lean of a motorcycle, and the layout and the exhaust of the engine sound. What the model is built from (the suspension, the driveline, the soft body, the size) builds the car again where it drives, half a second after the last change (`Car.rebuild`: the physics keeps its place and speed); the rest works at once.
